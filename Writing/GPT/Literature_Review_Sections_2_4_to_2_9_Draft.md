## 2.4 Synthetic Aperture Radar for Plastic Detection

The limitations of optical monitoring motivate the investigation of active microwave sensors. Optical imagery has provided the clearest satellite observations of floating debris to date, but its availability is constrained by cloud cover, daylight and atmospheric conditions. These restrictions are particularly consequential in coastal environments, where debris distributions can change between a clear optical observation and a later satellite acquisition. Synthetic aperture radar (SAR) offers a complementary observation capability because it supplies its own illumination, operates by day and night and is generally able to image through cloud. SAR therefore has the potential to improve the temporal consistency of marine litter monitoring. Its value, however, does not arise from a unique electromagnetic signature of plastic. Instead, SAR records how floating material and associated surface films alter the geometry and roughness of the water surface. Understanding this indirect relationship is necessary before SAR observations can be used to create labels or train a detection model.

### 2.4.1 Synthetic Aperture Radar Overview

Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) is an active, coherent microwave imaging system. It illuminates the surface with transmitted pulses and records the signal scattered back towards the antenna. Before detection, each focused image sample is complex-valued: its magnitude describes the strength of the received field and its phase describes the position of the returned wave relative to a reference. Calibrated detected power can subsequently be expressed as a backscatter coefficient such as sigma nought. The "synthetic aperture" is formed by coherently combining echoes collected from successive antenna positions along the satellite track. This produces an effective aperture much longer than the physical antenna and permits fine azimuth resolution from an orbital platform (Hajnsek and Desnos, 2021; Simpson, 2024).

Because SAR supplies its own illumination, acquisition is possible during both day and night. Microwave propagation also makes C-band SAR largely insensitive to the cloud and haze that obscure optical imagery. It is nevertheless more accurate to describe SAR as *nearly all-weather* than as completely independent of weather. Intense precipitation can affect microwave propagation and, over water, wind, rainfall and currents can alter the surface roughness that generates the measured return. SAR therefore improves observational continuity under cloud cover, but it does not make the target response independent of meteorological conditions (Hajnsek and Desnos, 2021; Simpson, 2024).

The appearance of a target in a SAR image depends on the interaction among wavelength, incidence angle, polarisation, surface geometry, dielectric properties and roughness relative to the wavelength. Common spaceborne systems operate at X-, C- or L-band, with shorter wavelengths interacting more strongly with small surface structures and longer wavelengths generally penetrating further into vegetation or other weakly attenuating media. Wavelength should not, however, be treated as a direct determinant of spatial resolution. Range resolution is principally governed by transmitted bandwidth, while azimuth resolution depends on the synthetic aperture, acquisition mode and focusing process. A long-wavelength sensor can therefore provide fine spatial resolution, and a short-wavelength sensor can be operated in a coarse-resolution wide-swath mode. Sentinel-1 uses C-band radar at approximately 5.405 GHz, corresponding to a wavelength of about 5.6 cm. This wavelength is relevant to centimetre-scale roughness and structure at the water surface, whereas the actual image resolution is determined by the selected Sentinel-1 mode and product processing (European Space Agency, 2023).

#### Polarisation and information content

Polarisation describes the orientation of the transmitted and received electric fields. A co-polarised channel uses the same orientation on transmission and reception, as in VV or HH, whereas a cross-polarised channel uses orthogonal orientations, as in VH or HV. Sentinel-1 commonly records VV and VH together over land and many coastal regions: VV denotes vertical transmission and vertical reception, while VH denotes vertical transmission and horizontal reception. A single-polarisation acquisition records one channel; a dual-polarisation acquisition records two channels, commonly one co-polarised and one cross-polarised return; and a quad-polarisation acquisition nominally records HH, HV, VH and VV. For a reciprocal monostatic target, HV and VH are expected to be approximately equal after calibration, but both measurements form part of a fully polarimetric observation.

These modes should not be arranged into a universal hierarchy in which one is always better for a particular surface. A co-polarised channel may provide a strong response to rough water, soil or oriented structures, while a cross-polarised return can add sensitivity to depolarising interactions associated with complex geometry, multiple scattering or volume structure. Cross-polarised ocean returns are often weaker and can approach the sensor noise floor, so the value of the additional channel depends on target contrast, incidence angle, sea state and radiometric quality. Dual-polarisation has improved discrimination in several vegetation and target-classification studies, but this is an application-dependent benefit rather than proof that dual-polarisation must outperform a well-chosen single channel (Hajnsek and Desnos, 2021; Mandal et al., 2020).

The two intensities can also be combined into a polarisation ratio. For calibrated linear-power measurements, this may be written as

$$
R_{\mathrm{VH/VV}}=\frac{\sigma^0_{\mathrm{VH}}}{\sigma^0_{\mathrm{VV}}},
$$

or, in decibels, as the difference $\sigma^0_{\mathrm{VH,dB}}-\sigma^0_{\mathrm{VV,dB}}$. The ratio can reduce multiplicative intensity variation shared by both channels and emphasise their relative response, although it remains sensitive to a weak or noise-limited denominator. Importantly, VH/VV is a *polarisation ratio*, not a cross-correlation coefficient. Cross-correlation requires the complex channel measurements and is represented by an off-diagonal covariance term, as defined below.

Quad-polarisation data support a more complete description of the scattering matrix and enable full-polarimetric decompositions. Such decompositions use statistical models or eigenanalysis to represent measured scattering as combinations of components or mechanisms. They do not identify a material unambiguously or reveal a literal dominant mechanism for every individual speckled pixel; their interpretation depends on the decomposition assumptions, calibration and spatial averaging. Sentinel-1 does not acquire the full quad-polarimetric matrix. Consequently, descriptors obtained from its VV/VH data contain useful but incomplete polarimetric information and must not be interpreted as if all four channels had been measured (Hajnsek and Desnos, 2021; Ji and Wu, 2015).

#### Sentinel-1 SLC and GRD products

Single Look Complex (SLC) and Ground Range Detected (GRD) data are both focused Sentinel-1 Level-1 products; neither is an unprocessed radar image. An SLC product preserves complex in-phase and quadrature samples, and therefore magnitude and phase, in slant-range geometry. It is single-look in each image dimension and retains the full signal bandwidth available for its acquisition mode. A GRD product is formed by detecting the focused signal, applying multilooking and projecting the image into ground-range geometry using an Earth ellipsoid. GRD pixels contain detected magnitude information from which calibrated intensity or backscatter can be derived, but they no longer retain the complex phase (European Space Agency, 2023).

Multilooking averages approximately independent looks. It reduces speckle variance and improves radiometric stability, but sacrifices spatial resolution; it does not remove speckle completely. The approximately square ground-range sampling of a GRD product results from the combined detection, multilooking, ground projection and resampling workflow rather than from multilooking alone. GRD is consequently smaller and simpler to use for intensity-based mapping. SLC requires more storage and processing, and its slant-range sampling, burst structure and complex data require appropriate calibration, debursting and geometric correction. The practical choice is therefore not simply "more information versus less information", but whether the expected gain from coherent or polarimetric features justifies the additional processing and whether those features are valid for the target and acquisition.

Preserving phase in an SLC product enables operations that are impossible with GRD, but several distinct concepts must be separated. Complex VV and VH samples acquired simultaneously can be combined to estimate *polarimetric covariance* and relative cross-channel phase. In contrast, conventional interferometric coherence compares the phase of two spatially co-registered complex acquisitions and therefore requires an image pair. An SLC file makes both forms of processing possible, but a single SLC acquisition does not by itself provide temporal interferometric coherence.

This distinction also refines the interpretation of Simpson et al. (2022). Their river study compared intensity-only change detectors with coherent polarimetric change detectors that used the complex dual-channel covariance information from Sentinel-1 SLC data. The best coherent detectors distinguished large, dam-trapped plastic accumulations from reference conditions more effectively than the tested intensity-only alternatives, and VH intensity was more discriminative than VV in that particular setting. This is important evidence that complex and cross-polarised information can assist plastic-accumulation detection, but it is not a general demonstration that SLC always detects smaller targets or that standard interferometric coherence directly detects floating plastic. Their result was obtained for large river accumulations, specific change-reference images and a constrained false-alarm analysis; transfer to dispersed coastal or open-ocean debris must therefore be tested rather than assumed.

#### Spatial texture

Intensity and polarisation ratios describe individual pixels or channel relationships, but potential debris signatures may also be spatially organised as patches, streaks, fronts or windrows. Texture features attempt to quantify this neighbourhood structure. A grey-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) counts how frequently a quantised grey level $i$ occurs with grey level $j$ at a specified displacement and direction within a local window. After normalisation, the matrix represents a joint probability distribution $P(i,j)$ from which statistical texture measures can be calculated (Haralick et al., 1973).

The present processing chain retains three VV GLCM-derived candidates. GLCM mean is a probability-weighted measure of the local grey-level distribution represented by the matrix. GLCM standard deviation, calculated in the project as the square root of the GLCM variance, describes dispersion in that distribution. GLCM entropy,

$$
H_{\mathrm{GLCM}}=-\sum_i\sum_j P(i,j)\log P(i,j),
$$

is low when a small number of grey-level pairings dominate and increases as the local pair distribution becomes less ordered. These measures may help distinguish a spatially coherent accumulation or convergence line from comparatively uniform background water even when their mean intensities overlap. They may also describe boundaries or patchiness that a single-pixel classifier would miss.

Texture is not intrinsically target-specific. Waves, rain cells, current fronts, biological slicks, ships, wakes, shoreline clutter and residual speckle can all alter local spatial statistics. GLCM values also depend on the intensity scale, quantisation, window size, displacement, direction, filtering and image resolution. The project therefore computes the VV texture layers from a speckle-filtered image using a defined local configuration, while retaining unfiltered calibrated backscatter as a separate input. The exact settings belong in the methods chapter; in the literature review the important point is that texture provides candidate spatial context, whose contribution must be established by validation or feature-ablation experiments rather than presumed from its definition.

#### Dual-polarisation covariance and eigenvalue descriptors

For a calibrated VV/VH SLC acquisition, the complex scattering vector can be written as

$$
\mathbf{k}=
\begin{bmatrix}
S_{\mathrm{VV}}\\
S_{\mathrm{VH}}
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The local dual-polarisation covariance matrix is then

$$
\mathbf{C}_2
=\left\langle \mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{H}\right\rangle
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\left\langle |S_{\mathrm{VV}}|^2\right\rangle &
\left\langle S_{\mathrm{VV}}S_{\mathrm{VH}}^{*}\right\rangle\\
\left\langle S_{\mathrm{VH}}S_{\mathrm{VV}}^{*}\right\rangle &
\left\langle |S_{\mathrm{VH}}|^2\right\rangle
\end{bmatrix},
$$

where $H$ denotes conjugate transpose, $*$ denotes complex conjugation and the angle brackets denote local or ensemble averaging. Averaging is required because a single coherent pixel is affected by speckle and cannot provide a stable covariance estimate. The diagonal elements $C_{11}$ and $C_{22}$ are the locally averaged VV and VH powers. The off-diagonal term $C_{12}$ is the complex VV-VH cross-covariance and $C_{21}=C_{12}^{*}$. A normalised complex correlation can be formed as

$$
\rho_{\mathrm{VV,VH}}=
\frac{C_{12}}{\sqrt{C_{11}C_{22}}},
$$

which is fundamentally different from the intensity ratio $C_{22}/C_{11}$. The covariance formulation is desirable because it preserves how the channel magnitudes and their relative phase vary together, adding information unavailable in separate GRD intensity layers (Mandal et al., 2020; Simpson et al., 2022).

Eigenanalysis expresses the Hermitian covariance matrix as

$$
\mathbf{C}_2=\mathbf{U}
\begin{bmatrix}
\lambda_1&0\\
0&\lambda_2
\end{bmatrix}
\mathbf{U}^{H}, \qquad \lambda_1\geq\lambda_2\geq0,
$$

with normalised eigenvalue probabilities $p_i=\lambda_i/(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)$. The project uses SNAP's dual-polarisation H-alpha implementation to export three descriptors from this local matrix:

- **Dual-polarisation entropy**, $H_2=-\sum_{i=1}^{2}p_i\log_2p_i$, ranges from 0 to 1. Values near zero indicate that one eigencomponent dominates the measured dual-channel response; values near one indicate that the two eigenvalues contribute more equally. This is polarimetric eigenvalue entropy and is distinct from spatial GLCM entropy.
- **Dual-polarisation anisotropy**, as labelled by the processing software, is an eigenvalue contrast that can be written $A_2=(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)/(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)$. It approaches one when one component dominates and zero when the eigenvalues are equal. This two-eigenvalue quantity is also closely related to the degree of polarisation. It is not the classical full-polarimetric Cloude-Pottier anisotropy, which is defined from the second and third eigenvalues of a three-component coherency matrix. Because both $H_2$ and $A_2$ depend only on the same two normalised eigenvalues, they are mathematically related rather than fully independent features.
- **Mean alpha angle**, $\bar{\alpha}_2$, is calculated from the covariance eigenvectors and their eigenvalue weights. If $\mathbf{u}_i$ is the eigenvector associated with $\lambda_i$, the implementation obtains an angle from its first component, $\alpha_i=\cos^{-1}(|u_{i,1}|)$, and reports $\bar{\alpha}_2=p_1\alpha_1+p_2\alpha_2$ in degrees. It summarises the orientation or mixing of the dominant states in the measured two-channel basis. In full-polarimetric analysis, alpha is often related to canonical surface, dipole/volume and double-bounce regimes. That direct interpretation is substantially weaker for a VV/VH pair because the second co-polarised channel is absent.

This final limitation is particularly important for the present study. Ji and Wu (2015) showed that dual co-polarisation data such as HH/VV retain more capacity to separate canonical scattering responses than co-/cross-polarised pairs such as HH/HV or VV/VH. The Sentinel-1 VV/VH H-alpha outputs should therefore be described as *partial, basis-dependent dual-polarisation descriptors*, not as a full decomposition that uniquely assigns surface, volume or double-bounce scattering to each pixel. Their value in this project is empirical: they compactly summarise local cross-channel structure that is absent from VV, VH and their ratio, and can be tested as candidate predictors. Feature importance, ablation against intensity-only baselines and validation across sites are required to establish whether that additional information is robust or merely correlated with sea state, noise or scene context.

The resulting feature set deliberately spans complementary levels of description: calibrated VV and VH represent channel-specific backscatter; their ratio represents relative polarisation response; GLCM layers represent local spatial organisation; and the covariance-derived layers represent local complex dual-channel structure. None is expected to be a unique signature of plastic. Their scientific relevance depends on the physical pathways through which floating material, convergent flow and associated surface films modify ocean roughness and scattering. These pathways are therefore considered next.

### 2.4.2 Ocean Surface Physics in SAR Imagery

Open water is not represented by a constant radar value. A very smooth surface reflects much of the incident energy away from a side-looking radar and therefore appears dark. Wind generates short capillary and gravity waves, increasing the roughness of the surface. At appropriate wavelengths and viewing geometries, resonant scattering from these waves increases the returned power and produces a brighter ocean background. Breaking waves, whitecaps and steep wave facets can add stronger and less polarised returns. Consequently, the backscatter measured over water varies with wind speed and direction, wave state, incidence angle, viewing azimuth and polarisation (Hajnsek and Desnos, 2021; Simpson, 2024).

Several scattering mechanisms may contribute within a coastal pixel. Surface scattering dominates where the return is controlled by water roughness. A vertical or inclined object next to the water surface can generate a strong geometric return, including double-bounce scattering between the object and the water. A disordered mixture of wet, partially submerged or vegetated material can produce multiple scattering paths and cross-polarised energy. Conversely, oils and surfactant films damp the short waves responsible for much of the radar return, producing dark slicks relative to the surrounding water. Ermakov et al. (2022) showed that film slicks suppress both Bragg and non-polarised components of sea-surface backscatter, with the observed contrast also depending on wave direction and radar geometry.

Oceanographic processes further organise these scattering conditions. Fronts, river plumes, internal waves, tidal boundaries and Langmuir circulation can create bands of convergence and divergence. Convergence zones may accumulate buoyant natural material and anthropogenic litter into fronts or windrows, while simultaneously changing the local roughness of the water. Windrows are therefore both plausible accumulation zones and radar-visible physical structures in their own right (Ruiz et al., 2020; Aliani et al., 2022). A spatial association between a radar lineament and an expected accumulation zone is useful evidence, but it is not sufficient to identify the accumulated material as plastic.

This dependence on environmental state has two implications for a detection system. First, the same debris accumulation may have different contrast under different wind, wave and viewing conditions. Second, similar bright or dark features can occur without plastic. Wind and wave information can help describe the expected radar background, while currents and sea-surface temperature can indicate fronts, convergence and transport pathways. These variables should be used as contextual covariates rather than treated as direct evidence that plastic is present.

### 2.4.3 Floating Plastic and SAR Signal Returns

The literature does not support a single, invariant SAR signature for floating plastic. Observed responses can be separated into at least two physical pathways. Large, dense or geometrically complex accumulations may increase local roughness or introduce additional scattering elements, causing a brighter return than nearby calm water. By contrast, microplastic distributions have sometimes been inferred through the presence of associated surfactants that damp short surface waves and create dark anomalies. These pathways describe different target scales and mechanisms and should not be combined into a universal "plastic backscatter" assumption.

Evidence for a bright response is strongest under controlled conditions or where debris is densely accumulated. Simpson et al. (2022) examined plastic islands trapped at river barriers using Sentinel-1 data. The accumulations increased local roughness and were detectable in both GRD and SLC observations, with VH and SLC-derived polarimetric power differences providing particularly useful discrimination. Their strongest detectors achieved high positive-detection rates at a low false-alarm level in the studied river scenes. The result demonstrates that large accumulations can affect a spaceborne C-band signal, but river barriers, banks and persistent target locations make the setting different from an open coastal ocean.

Controlled radar experiments clarify the role of target and environmental conditions. De Fockert et al. (2024) found that the detectability of floating plastic depended on item type, size, concentration and wave height. Under their freshwater tank conditions, X- and Ku-band VV observations produced the clearest responses, with detectability emerging above concentration ranges specific to the experiment. Increased wave height reduced the contrast of smaller targets. Nunziata et al. (2026) similarly observed a brighter return from controlled floating plastic targets than from calm lake water in X-band co- and cross-polarised data. In that experiment, HH provided the strongest target-water separation. However, plastic was not straightforward to distinguish from natural foliage using intensity and multipolarisation alone. Both studies show that radar can respond to floating plastic, but both also caution against transferring a threshold measured in calm, controlled water directly to C-band satellite imagery of a rough marine surface.

Spaceborne observations reinforce this variability. Qi et al. (2022) reported that dense macroalgae produced a comparatively consistent bright response in Sentinel-1 observations, whereas marine debris exhibited both bright and dark responses. This variability is physically plausible: the observed return depends not only on the polymer but also on the geometry, wetness, packing density and motion of the accumulated material, as well as the surrounding sea state. A plastic target may therefore be detectable because it changes the effective roughness of the surface, not because the radar directly identifies its chemical composition.

A different branch of the literature uses radar-derived roughness suppression as an indirect proxy for microplastic. Davaasuren et al. (2018) associated extensive dark features in SAR imagery with hypothesised surfactant-rich microplastic accumulation zones. Evans and Ruf (2022) used CYGNSS bistatic radar observations to estimate anomalous reductions in ocean-surface roughness that may be associated with surfactants and microplastic concentration. These studies are important for broad-scale monitoring, but the inferred quantity is not a direct observation of individual plastic objects. Low wind, natural films, oil and other processes can also suppress short waves. The resulting products should therefore be described as roughness-based proxies or candidate accumulation indicators, not as confirmed plastic labels.

Together, these studies suggest that SAR contributes several kinds of evidence: local intensity contrast, polarimetric behaviour, texture and the spatial form of fronts or accumulations. No individual feature is expected to be sufficient across all scenes. This motivates a model that can learn combinations of physically interpretable SAR features while using environmental context to account for changing background conditions.

### 2.4.4 Lookalikes and Sources of False Detection

False detections are central to the SAR plastic-detection problem because most candidate signatures are not material-specific. Dark anomalies can result from low wind, natural biogenic films, mineral oil, current shadows or sheltered water. Bright anomalies can be produced by ships, wakes, breaking waves, rain cells, coastal infrastructure, aquaculture structures, macroalgae and other natural floating debris. Fronts and windrows can appear as linear roughness boundaries even when they contain little or no anthropogenic material. Shorelines and harbours are particularly complex because strong land returns, layover, vessel traffic and mixed land-water pixels occur close to likely litter sources.

Natural organic material presents a difficult target-level confusion. The controlled X-band results of Nunziata et al. (2026) show that floating plastic and foliage may both brighten otherwise calm water. Qi et al. (2022) likewise found that dense macroalgae can generate a strong SAR response. A classifier trained only on positive plastic examples and generic open-water negatives may therefore learn "floating accumulation" rather than plastic. Ships and wakes create a related problem: they are visually distinctive at some scales but can be confused with small bright patches or linear accumulations after resampling and patch extraction.

The training data should consequently contain explicit hard-negative classes rather than a single undifferentiated background. At minimum, these should include vessels and wakes, macroalgae or natural debris, fronts and windrows, slicks, rain- or wave-related roughness, harbour infrastructure and representative low-wind water. Environmental data can further constrain interpretation. Wind is the most direct contextual variable for expected radar roughness; waves describe the sea-state background; currents and sea-surface temperature can identify transport structures and convergence. None of these variables proves that plastic is present, but together they can help a model separate a physically plausible target from a background artefact.

### 2.4.5 Detectability Limits and Sentinel-1 Constraints

The experimental literature establishes physical feasibility, but it does not define a transferable minimum target for Sentinel-1. Detectability depends on the contrast between a target-affected area and the surrounding water, not simply on the total mass of plastic present. An accumulation must either occupy a sufficient part of the radar resolution cell, contain scattering elements large and suitably oriented relative to the wavelength, or modify the water roughness over a sufficiently large area. Dispersed items below the effective resolution may be invisible even when their total number is environmentally important. Conversely, a thin surface film can affect a broad area of short-wave roughness without containing a dense macroplastic aggregation. Target area, concentration and mechanism must therefore be reported separately.

Frequency is one reason that controlled results cannot be transferred directly. De Fockert et al. (2024) reported detection at approximately 1 item per square metre or 1-10 g per square metre using X- and Ku-band VV measurements under the conditions of their hydrodynamic facility. They also measured much smaller contrast at C-band than at X-band for one high-concentration plastic-sphere experiment. Their study explicitly excluded wind, and the authors note that the capillary-wave portion of the experimental spectrum was not necessarily representative of the open ocean. Nunziata et al. (2026) used X-band Spotlight imagery with sub-metre sampling over calm lake water. The experiment provides strong evidence that a known macroplastic aggregation can be visible from a satellite, but its very fine resolution, HH/HV configuration, frequency and low-clutter setting differ materially from Sentinel-1 Interferometric Wide Swath observations. These results define mechanisms and boundary conditions; they do not supply a C-band ocean threshold.

The Sentinel-1 result of Simpson et al. (2022) is more directly comparable in frequency and product type. Their river study used a multi-year sequence of 142 Interferometric Wide Swath SLC acquisitions and reported that the best coherent power-difference and VH difference detectors achieved approximately 85-95% positive detections at a 0.1% false-alarm rate across the studied accumulations. The best GRD-compatible VH difference detector achieved approximately 70-90% under the same false-alarm criterion. These figures are important, but they describe change detection at persistent plastic traps near dams, where reference dates and target locations were available. They should not be quoted as expected accuracy for a single-scene, open-coast segmentation model. Their value for this study is the narrower conclusion that cross-polarised and coherent information may contain useful target-background contrast beyond VV intensity alone.

Acquisition geometry also changes the background against which a target is detected. Backscatter normally varies with incidence angle, and azimuthal relationships between the radar look direction, wind and wave field can alter ocean contrast. Ascending and descending passes may therefore observe the same area differently. Calibration and incidence-angle normalisation reduce some systematic variation, but they cannot make physically different sea states equivalent. Training and test data should retain acquisition metadata so that performance can be stratified by orbit direction, incidence-angle range and environmental condition rather than averaged into a single unexplained score.

Several processing effects are particularly relevant to a small-target study. Speckle is produced by coherent interference among many scatterers within a resolution cell. Spatial averaging or texture windows can stabilise estimates, but aggressive filtering can blur a narrow windrow or merge it with the surrounding water. Thermal noise and low cross-polarised signal can reduce the reliability of VH-derived features in dark water. Mixed land-water pixels, geometric distortion and resampling can create strong coastal gradients close to ports and river mouths, precisely where plastic inputs are plausible. These effects do not make coastal SAR unusable, but they require exclusion masks, quality flags or dedicated negative examples.

SLC phase must also be interpreted carefully. Preserving complex data enables covariance estimation and coherent feature construction, but a moving, decorrelating water surface does not provide a stable phase reference in the same way as a static solid target. Polarimetric and textural descriptors are estimated over finite neighbourhoods, creating a trade-off between statistical stability and spatial detail. The project should therefore test whether each SLC-derived feature adds repeatable information through ablation rather than assuming that a physically sophisticated descriptor is automatically predictive.

The practical outcome is a conditional concept of detectability. A candidate accumulation is most likely to be observable when it occupies enough of the radar footprint, produces contrast relative to the prevailing sea state and is not masked by stronger clutter. A non-detection does not demonstrate the absence of plastic, particularly for dispersed or sub-pixel material. Similarly, a strong anomaly is not confirmation without independent evidence. This bounded interpretation keeps the study aligned with its actual objective: segmenting candidate floating-plastic accumulations under specified conditions and uncertainty, rather than estimating all plastic present in the coastal ocean.

This discussion changes the modelling question. The objective is not to find one threshold that identifies plastic in every SAR scene. It is to segment candidate accumulations from a spatially and physically variable background, test which feature groups add transferable information and preserve uncertainty where the evidence cannot distinguish plastic from a lookalike. The following section considers the machine-learning architectures and training strategies required for that task.

## 2.5 Machine Learning

The interaction between floating material and the radar-observed ocean surface is non-linear and context dependent. Fixed thresholds or a small number of hand-crafted decision rules are unlikely to accommodate changes in wind, sea state, incidence angle, target concentration and coastline complexity. Machine learning offers a means of learning joint patterns across backscatter, polarimetric, textural and environmental variables. Its value depends, however, on selecting a prediction task and validation design that reflect the scientific objective.

Pixel-wise segmentation is more appropriate than image-level classification for this study. A patch classifier can determine whether a scene contains a candidate feature, but it does not locate the feature precisely enough to compare it with an observed or drift-adjusted label. Semantic segmentation instead assigns a class probability to each pixel, retaining target shape and location and supporting overlap-based evaluation. This distinction is important when plastic occupies only a small fraction of a coastal SAR patch.

### 2.5.1 Model Architectures

Early remote-sensing detection studies commonly used shallow classifiers such as random forests, support vector machines and naive Bayes models. Savastano et al. (2021) provided an early SAR-focused plastic-detection pilot by transferring near-coincident optical information to Sentinel-1 and applying random forest, Gaussian naive Bayes and support vector machine classifiers. The study demonstrated the feasibility of relating Sentinel-1 features to optical observations, but its limited binary dataset and shallow pixel classification did not resolve the wider problems of spatial context, label uncertainty and domain transfer.

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) learn spatial filters directly from image data. Their local receptive fields make them suitable for texture, edges and compact objects, while deeper layers combine these features over a larger spatial area. Residual networks introduce skip connections that allow information and gradients to pass across groups of layers, supporting deeper feature extractors without the same degree of optimisation degradation. A ResNet-style encoder could therefore learn strong patch-level representations, but a classification network alone would still produce only an image label unless coupled to a decoder.

U-Net addresses this limitation through an encoder-decoder structure. The encoder progressively extracts semantic features at reduced spatial resolution, while the decoder restores the spatial detail required for a dense output. Skip connections transfer high-resolution features from the encoder to corresponding decoder stages. In optical marine-debris studies, U-Net-type models have consistently provided a practical foundation for segmentation. Mifdal et al. (2021) found that learned spatial features outperformed pixel-wise shallow methods on Sentinel-2 floating-object data, while work using MARIDA has shown that U-Net variants can learn a rare debris class within a multiclass coastal scene (Kikaki et al., 2022; Bojesomo et al., 2023).

An Attention U-Net extends this architecture by applying attention gates to the skip connections. These gates use coarser contextual information to suppress irrelevant encoder activations and retain features associated with the target (Oktay et al., 2018). This is attractive for coastal plastic detection because most pixels are background and many strong radar features are unrelated to debris. Attention may help the decoder focus on a small candidate accumulation while still using surrounding fronts, water texture and coastline context. It does not remove the need for representative negative examples: an attention mechanism trained on biased labels can still attend to a vessel, shoreline or slick.

Transformer-based architectures offer another way to represent long-range spatial relationships. Their self-attention mechanisms can connect distant parts of a scene and may be useful for extended fronts or windrows. Their data and computational requirements are less well matched to the small labelled SAR dataset expected in this project, particularly if trained from random initialisation. Hybrid CNN-transformer models or transformer encoders may become useful once larger labelled datasets are available, but a compact Attention U-Net provides a more defensible primary model for the present study. It combines dense prediction, multi-scale context and a manageable parameter count while allowing the encoder to be initialised through self-supervised learning.

The architectural justification should remain comparative rather than absolute. Attention U-Net was developed and validated for medical-image segmentation, not for SAR plastic detection (Oktay et al., 2018). Its attention gates are relevant because they filter skip-connection features using coarser contextual information and can suppress irrelevant activations with limited additional computational cost. Whether this mechanism improves the present task is an empirical question. A conventional U-Net with the same encoder and training data is therefore an essential baseline. Comparing it with the Attention U-Net isolates the contribution of the gates, while a shallow classifier or patch-level CNN provides a lower-complexity reference. Without such controls, any improvement could be caused by a different encoder, loss, training schedule or input feature set rather than attention itself.

Model capacity should also be matched to evidence quality. Bojesomo et al. (2023) showed on MARIDA that a parameter-efficient octonion-based architecture could reduce parameter count substantially while maintaining competitive segmentation performance. This does not establish that an octonion representation is required for SAR, but it reinforces a broader point: larger models are not automatically preferable when labels are scarce. A compact architecture reduces the opportunity to memorise acquisition-specific artefacts and makes repeated ablation across feature groups and study locations more feasible.

### 2.5.2 Training Strategies

The largest limitation is not the absence of increasingly complex architectures but the scarcity and uncertainty of labels. Rußwurm et al. (2023) showed in the optical domain that dataset harmonisation, negative sampling and label refinement can affect performance more strongly than substituting one high-capacity architecture for another. A training strategy for SAR plastic detection should therefore prioritise the quality and structure of the evidence presented to the model.

Data augmentation can increase the effective variety of a small training set. Flips, 90-degree rotations and spatial crops are plausible where the target has no preferred image orientation. Controlled intensity perturbations and noise injection may improve robustness to calibration and acquisition variability. Augmentations must be applied consistently across related SAR channels and should preserve physically meaningful inter-channel relationships. Arbitrary colour transformations designed for natural photographs are not appropriate for calibrated backscatter or polarimetric descriptors. Directional effects also require care: a rotation changes the image orientation of wind rows, azimuthal scattering and coastline geometry, even if it remains useful as a regulariser.

Self-supervised learning (SSL) can exploit the much larger collection of unlabelled Sentinel-1 scenes. In a contrastive approach, an encoder is trained to map related views of a SAR patch to nearby embeddings while separating unrelated observations. An embedding is a compact numerical representation in which patches with similar learned characteristics occupy nearby locations. The resulting encoder can then initialise the Attention U-Net and be fine-tuned with the smaller labelled dataset. Pei et al. (2023) demonstrated that contrastive self-supervision can improve SAR representations when downstream labels are limited, although their applications were not marine-plastic segmentation. Glaser et al. (2024) extended this principle at scale using almost ten million Sentinel-1 Wave Mode images and found that domain-specific SAR pretraining supported downstream classification and retrieval better than generic natural-image initialisation. For the present study, local self-supervised pretraining also offers a way to expose the encoder to the normal variation in South African coastal water before asking it to recognise a rare target.

Class imbalance must be handled at both sampling and loss levels. Plastic pixels will be rare relative to water, and randomly drawn patches may contain no useful positive signal. Training should combine target-centred patches with diverse background and hard-negative patches, including ships, natural material, slicks and coastal clutter. Weighted binary cross-entropy or focal loss can increase the contribution of rare and difficult pixels, while a Dice- or intersection-over-union-based term can directly reward spatial overlap. The operating threshold should be selected on validation data rather than assumed to be 0.5, and precision-recall behaviour should be reported because overall accuracy can remain high even when every plastic pixel is missed.

Spatial leakage is another major risk. Adjacent patches from the same acquisition are highly correlated, and random patch-level splitting can place nearly identical water texture or overlapping image areas in training and test sets. Mifdal et al. (2021) and Kikaki et al. (2022) emphasise geographically or scene-disjoint evaluation in optical debris datasets. The SAR dataset should similarly be divided by acquisition, event and location. Testing across Durban, Gqeberha and Cape Town can then measure geographic transfer rather than memorisation of a coastline or acquisition.

Finally, uncertain labels should not be presented as equally reliable hard truth. Direct synchronous observations, near-coincident optical transfer and drift-projected estimates have different evidential strength. Confidence weights, soft target probabilities or separate sensitivity analyses can retain these distinctions during training and evaluation. This requirement connects the modelling strategy to the available data and to the way labels are created.

The role of augmentation in this workflow should also be verified rather than assumed. Glaser et al. (2024) found that domain knowledge in augmentation design affected the usefulness of contrastively learned Sentinel-1 Wave Mode representations. For coastal SLC-derived inputs, an augmentation is valid only if it preserves the relationship that the downstream model is expected to learn. The same geometric transformation should be applied to every aligned SAR and label channel. Intensity changes should respect calibrated units and should not independently alter VV, VH and derived ratios in a way that creates impossible polarimetric combinations. If wind direction or radar look geometry is included as context, a rotated image requires the directional metadata to be transformed consistently or the rotation should be excluded.

Self-supervised pretraining must likewise be evaluated as a transfer strategy, not treated as guaranteed improvement. Pei et al. (2023) demonstrated a two-stage approach in which a CNN encoder learned a discriminative embedding from unlabelled SAR data and was then fine-tuned with relatively few labels. Glaser et al. (2024) showed that a domain-specific Sentinel-1 model outperformed comparable ImageNet pretraining on several ocean-related downstream tasks and retrieval, particularly when labelled data were limited. Neither study addressed floating-plastic segmentation. The appropriate test is consequently a controlled comparison among random initialisation, generic image pretraining where channel compatibility permits it, and local SAR self-supervision. If the locally pretrained model improves held-out scenes rather than only the training set, the result would support the claim that embeddings capture transferable coastal SAR structure.

### 2.5.3 Environmental and Multimodal Data Fusion

Environmental variables can enter the study in three distinct roles: describing the radar background, supporting label transport and acting as model inputs. These roles must not be conflated. Wind and wave state affect surface roughness and therefore the interpretation of SAR backscatter. Currents, fronts and temperature gradients can indicate transport and convergence. The same current and wind fields may also drive the ensemble used to project a source observation to the SAR acquisition time. A variable used in label construction is not automatically independent predictive evidence.

The simplest fusion strategy is early fusion, in which environmental rasters are resampled to the SAR grid and stacked with the image channels. This makes all variables available to one encoder and is straightforward to implement. It can, however, imply a spatial precision that the environmental product does not possess. A current or wind field with kilometre-scale resolution should not be interpreted as having independent information at every SAR pixel after interpolation. Repeating the same coarse value across many fine pixels changes the array dimensions but not the information content.

Later fusion preserves this distinction more explicitly. A SAR branch can learn fine spatial features while a separate branch represents scene-level or coarse environmental conditions; their features are combined at a deeper stage or immediately before prediction. An alternative is not to provide environmental variables to the network at all, but to use them for stratified analysis. For example, a SAR-only model can be evaluated separately under low, moderate and high wind conditions. This approach tests whether the image model is robust to the environment without allowing it to use the environmental product as a shortcut.

The literature supports the relevance of context but not one universally optimal fusion design. Evans and Ruf (2022) required an independent wind estimate to calculate the difference between observed and wind-expected ocean roughness. Davaasuren et al. (2018) restricted and contextualised dark-feature interpretation using wind and ancillary evidence. Tran et al. (2025) used wind, waves, currents, temperature and salinity in an OpenDrift transport study, while Cernian and Iliuta (2025) combined detected debris locations with current and wind fields for trajectory prediction. These applications show that environmental information can change the meaning or expected location of a remote-sensing feature. They do not demonstrate that stacking all variables into a segmentation model will improve plastic discrimination.

A staged ablation is therefore the most scientifically defensible design. A first configuration should use calibrated VV and VH backscatter. A second should add inter-polarisation, texture and dual-polarisation descriptors. A third can add selected environmental context using a documented fusion strategy. Comparing these configurations on identical scene-level splits tests whether additional information improves held-out performance. A fourth analysis can retain SAR-only prediction and use the environmental variables solely to explain successes and failures. If the environmental-input model performs better only on scenes used in label projection, the possibility of leakage or event memorisation must be investigated.

Temporal alignment is as important as spatial alignment. SAR represents conditions during a specific acquisition, whereas winds, waves and currents may be hourly analyses, forecasts or daily averages. Each environmental field should be matched to the radar time using a stated tolerance and interpolation rule. Missing or low-quality environmental data require an explicit mask rather than silent filling. These safeguards prevent the model from treating data availability patterns, forecast cycles or coastline gaps as target cues.

### 2.5.4 Generalisation, Calibration and Evaluation

The central performance question is whether a model trained on a limited set of events can identify candidate accumulations in genuinely unseen conditions. Patch-level performance alone cannot answer this question. Patches from one Sentinel-1 acquisition share calibration, speckle statistics, environmental state and coastline context. Overlapping patches can also contain many of the same pixels. A random patch split therefore estimates interpolation within familiar scenes and can substantially overstate geographic generalisation. Mifdal et al. (2021) used region-wise separation because their dynamically cropped examples overlapped, and they identified domain shift between regions and acquisitions as a remaining challenge. MARIDA retained each unique scene and date within one split rather than randomly distributing its patches (Kikaki et al., 2022).

The hierarchy of evaluation units should be explicit. A patch-level split tests the narrowest form of interpolation and is unsuitable as the primary result. A scene-level split tests transfer to a new acquisition, while an event-level split prevents repeated observations of the same accumulation from appearing in training and test data. A location-level split, in which one of Durban, Gqeberha or Cape Town is held out, gives the strongest available test of geographic transfer. Because the three areas differ in coastline form, pollution sources and oceanographic regime, lower performance on a held-out city would be scientifically informative rather than merely a model failure.

Metrics must reflect both class rarity and spatial usefulness. Overall pixel accuracy is dominated by water and may remain high for a model that predicts no plastic. Receiver operating characteristic area under the curve can also appear favourable when true negatives are extremely numerous. Precision, recall, F1 score, intersection over union and the precision-recall curve are more directly related to the rare positive class. These metrics answer different questions: recall measures how much labelled target is recovered, precision measures how much predicted target overlaps the label, and intersection over union penalises both omission and over-segmentation.

Pixel metrics should be supplemented by scene-level quantities. The number of false candidate regions per scene or per unit water area describes the manual review burden. Object- or event-level recall can indicate whether an accumulation was found at all, even if its uncertain boundary was not reproduced exactly. Centroid displacement and overlap with a confidence envelope are useful for drift-adjusted labels. Results should be reported per location, acquisition and relevant environmental stratum before aggregation. A single mean can otherwise conceal a model that works only in calm water or one harbour.

Threshold selection is part of evaluation. The threshold used to convert a probability surface into a mask must be chosen on validation data and then fixed before testing. Reporting performance across the precision-recall curve avoids presenting one favourable operating point as the full result. Where output values are interpreted as confidence, probability calibration should also be examined. A reliability diagram or Brier score can show whether, for example, pixels assigned a probability near 0.8 are positive at anything close to that frequency. Calibration cannot make an uncertain label certain, but it can reveal overconfident predictions.

Uncertainty in the reference data places an upper bound on interpretable agreement. A prediction just outside a drift-projected core may be physically plausible, while a perfect match to a low-confidence polygon may not indicate genuine material discrimination. Primary evaluation should therefore use the highest-confidence labels, with secondary analyses adding lower-confidence or soft labels. Performance can be reported separately for Levels A, B and C of the proposed evidence hierarchy. This prevents a model from being rewarded or penalised as though all labels had equal epistemic status.

Finally, the evaluation should test causal claims about the workflow. Architecture ablation compares U-Net and Attention U-Net under otherwise fixed conditions. Feature ablation tests whether texture and dual-polarisation descriptors add value beyond VV/VH. Pretraining ablation compares random and self-supervised initialisation. Label ablation compares unadjusted, drift-adjusted and confidence-weighted training. These comparisons are more informative than an isolated headline score because they show which elements of the proposed framework are supported by evidence. Confidence intervals should be estimated over scenes or events rather than treating correlated pixels as independent samples.

Taken together, this design treats machine learning as a controlled scientific test. The model output is a probability of a candidate accumulation under the training definition, not a direct measurement of plastic mass or polymer type. Its validity depends on data provenance, independent evaluation and transparent failure analysis, which makes the available labelled evidence the next concern.

## 2.6 Available Labelled Data

Public marine-debris datasets have enabled substantial progress in optical detection, but they do not provide a ready-made benchmark for Sentinel-1 segmentation. "Labelled data" also describes several forms of evidence that should not be treated as equivalent. A polygon around a deployed target, an expert interpretation of a spectral anomaly, a line drawn through suspected debris, a bounding box around a high-resolution object and a drift-projected probability surface impose different levels of spatial precision and semantic certainty. Dataset size alone therefore does not determine usefulness for this study. Sensor compatibility, target definition, negative-class coverage, provenance and independence between training and test events are equally important.

The principal datasets and experimental resources relevant to this study are summarised below. Experimental radar campaigns are included even where they are not packaged as machine-learning benchmarks because they constrain the physical interpretation of any prospective SAR label.

Controlled optical projects provide the clearest relationship between a known target and a satellite observation. In Plastic Litter Project 2019, target composition and position were known and UAV observations supported interpretation of the Sentinel-2 signal (Topouzelis et al., 2020). Even in this controlled setting, wind, waves and currents moved or tilted the targets by metres, complicating the calculation of target coverage within a satellite pixel. The experiment therefore demonstrates both the value and the limitations of nominally known ground truth. A target location is not spatially exact merely because the deployment was controlled.

FloatingObjects and MARIDA contribute complementary annotation philosophies. FloatingObjects uses coarse line annotations for visually identified floating features across geographically separated Sentinel-2 scenes (Mifdal et al., 2021). The labels are efficient for elongated targets but do not define a precise debris boundary or guarantee material composition. Because patches cropped around line segments overlap, the study used region-wise data separation and still observed generalisation difficulties between sites and acquisitions. This experience is directly relevant to a patch library derived from larger SAR scenes.

MARIDA provides a richer multiclass alternative. It contains 1,381 patches and 837,357 annotated pixels from 63 Sentinel-2 scenes, but only 3,339 pixels are assigned to marine debris (Kikaki et al., 2022). Three expert annotators recorded confidence levels, and the released data preserve report information and annotation provenance. MARIDA also keeps each scene and unique date within a single train, validation or test partition. These decisions are at least as important to the present study as the optical spectra: they demonstrate how rare target pixels, realistic confusers, confidence and scene-level independence can be represented in a benchmark.

Rußwurm et al. (2023) show why multiple public datasets cannot simply be concatenated without reconciliation. FloatingObjects, MARIDA and ship-focused examples differ in spatial resolution, semantic definition and annotation geometry. Their data-centric workflow used extensive negative sampling and automated refinement of coarse labels before training a U-Net++ detector. The resulting lesson is not that one optical model should be copied for SAR, but that label harmonisation and hard-negative design can dominate small architectural differences. The NASA Marine Debris PlanetScope collection adds spatial detail, yet its RGB channels and coarse boxes again describe a different observation and target definition.

The radar resources address physical relevance but not benchmark scale. Simpson et al. (2022) provide verified Sentinel-1 observations of persistent accumulations and comparative SLC/GRD detection results, but the river barriers, reference-image design and change-detection labels do not reproduce open-coast segmentation. Savastano et al. (2021) provide one of the closest sensor combinations to this project, but the small transferred binary dataset does not capture the range of lookalikes or temporal uncertainty needed for a deep model. De Fockert et al. (2024) and Nunziata et al. (2026) provide stronger control over material, concentration and natural targets, but their frequencies, spatial scales and water conditions differ from Sentinel-1 coastal acquisitions. CYGNSS provides broad proxy coverage at a scale and semantic level unsuited to pixel labels for macroplastic.

Newer radar experiments consequently strengthen the case that floating plastic can influence a microwave return, but they do not remove the data gap. As of the sources consulted here, there is no established open dataset that combines multi-scene Sentinel-1 SLC features, pixel- or probability-level marine-plastic annotations and representative coastal confusers. The absence is more specific than a general shortage of marine-debris data: the missing resource is a radar-compatible, locally relevant and uncertainty-aware segmentation dataset.

Harmonisation should begin with an explicit target ontology. "Marine debris", "floating object", "suspected plastic", "macroplastic accumulation" and "microplastic proxy" are not interchangeable classes. The project target should be defined as a candidate surface accumulation consistent with the available confirming evidence at the Sentinel-1 acquisition time. Labels that identify an accumulation without confirming material may support an auxiliary "floating matter" class or manual candidate review, but should not silently become plastic positives. The same principle applies to proxy slicks.

Every sample should also preserve the unit from which it was derived. Patches must be linked to the source acquisition, event, location, orbit and label-generating observation. This allows all patches from one physical event to remain in one partition and prevents different dates or sensors representing the same event from leaking across training and test sets. Negative samples need equivalent provenance. Random open-water negatives are easy; scientifically useful negatives are vessels, wakes, natural accumulations, slicks, fronts and coastal artefacts observed under conditions in which the model will actually be used.

The table therefore reveals a mismatch between the available evidence and the intended prediction task. Optical datasets provide annotation and validation principles. Controlled radar experiments provide physical evidence. Sentinel-1 pilots demonstrate feasibility in restricted domains. None supplies a multi-scene South African dataset combining SLC-derived features, confirmed or uncertainty-aware labels, representative false positives and environmental context.

This gap means that label creation is part of the research contribution rather than a preliminary administrative step. Optical observations, field or UAV evidence and transport modelling can all support SAR labels, but their temporal and spatial uncertainty must remain visible in the dataset.

## 2.7 Label Creation and Spatiotemporal Alignment

A valid SAR label should refer to what was plausibly present at the time of the radar acquisition, not merely to a plastic observation made at another time. Floating debris is advected by surface currents and can also respond to wind drag, wave-induced Stokes drift, tides, fronts, turbulence and interactions with the coastline. Its movement depends on buoyancy, shape, size, submergence and aggregation state (van Sebille et al., 2020). Directly copying an optical polygon or field coordinate onto a SAR image acquired hours or days later can therefore introduce systematic displacement and teach a model to associate the wrong radar pixels with the target.

At least four uncertainty sources must be separated. **Observation uncertainty** concerns whether the source feature is actually plastic. **Geolocation uncertainty** concerns the positional accuracy of the source and SAR products. **Transport uncertainty** concerns the unknown path between acquisition times. **Boundary uncertainty** concerns which part of a mixed or elongated feature should be labelled. These errors have different remedies. Better image registration can reduce geolocation error but cannot confirm material type; a drift ensemble can represent transport uncertainty but cannot correct a false source classification; and a wider polygon can capture uncertain boundaries while also increasing the risk of labelling background water as positive.

The strongest labels are synchronous or near-synchronous observations in which debris is confirmed within the SAR acquisition window. UAV imagery, field observations or very close optical observations can provide this evidence if geolocation error and target movement are small relative to the SAR pixel and patch size. When the time separation is material, a Lagrangian trajectory model can estimate a more plausible target position. OpenDrift and related frameworks combine ocean currents with wind and wave forcing to simulate the movement of floating particles. Tran et al. (2025) demonstrate the use of currents, winds, waves and stochastic motion in debris-drift modelling, while also showing that transport varies with season and environmental forcing. Cernian and Iliuta (2025) similarly combine optical detection with Lagrangian transport to predict debris movement, although limited ground truth constrains quantitative validation.

The phrase "near-synchronous" should be defined by expected displacement rather than by an arbitrary number of hours. A short time difference during strong current, wind or wave forcing may produce more displacement than a longer interval in sheltered water. The acceptable interval also depends on target size and SAR resolution. If the combined registration and transport envelope remains small relative to the observed accumulation, direct transfer may be reasonable. If it is comparable to or larger than the target, the label should be downgraded or represented probabilistically.

Transport parameterisation should reflect object diversity. van Sebille et al. (2020) describe how buoyancy, shape, size, wind exposure, vertical mixing and terminal rise velocity affect floating-debris motion. Tran et al. (2025) note that wind-driven leeway is often parameterised as a fraction of wind speed, with values varying by object and exposure. Selecting one coefficient for all plastic would therefore create false precision. The ensemble should vary plausible leeway, release location and stochastic diffusion, and should include Stokes drift or wave effects where suitable forcing is available. Near the coast, beaching, harbour structures and unresolved circulation introduce additional uncertainty that may be larger than the nominal model spread.

A drift estimate should not be treated as an exact deterministic correction. Forecast or reanalysis currents have finite resolution, windage coefficients are object dependent, Stokes drift may be uncertain, and coastal interactions can dominate a trajectory near shore. An ensemble of trajectories that varies release position, windage and environmental forcing is more defensible than a single path. The ensemble can be rasterised as a probability surface or search envelope at the SAR acquisition time. A high-probability core can identify the most likely target region, while a lower-confidence band retains plausible alternatives. This representation is compatible with soft segmentation labels and avoids assigning full confidence to every pixel inside an uncertain projected polygon.

Forward and backward transfers require particular care. Forward modelling from an earlier confirmed observation to a later SAR time follows the normal trajectory direction. Transferring a later optical observation back to an earlier SAR acquisition is not simply the exact reversal of a diffusive forward simulation. Numerical diffusion, unresolved turbulence, convergence and beaching are not perfectly reversible. Backward trajectories can still define a search envelope, but the resulting label should generally carry lower confidence unless it is supported by observations on both sides of the SAR acquisition.

A practical soft-label construction can combine the source geometry with the ensemble endpoints. Each source pixel or sampled particle is propagated to the SAR time, and the endpoint density is accumulated on the SAR grid. The resulting density can be normalised into a relative probability surface, while registration uncertainty is represented by an additional spatial kernel. A high-density core can be used as the positive target, an outer plausible envelope as a soft or ignore region, and pixels beyond the envelope as candidate background subject to visual and contextual review. The probability should be described as a modelled location likelihood conditional on the source observation and forcing, not as the probability that the material is chemically plastic.

The dataset should preserve an explicit evidence hierarchy:

1. **Level A - direct confirmation:** synchronous in-situ, UAV or verified target-deployment evidence coincident with the SAR acquisition.
2. **Level B - near-coincident transfer:** optical or other observation close enough in time for displacement to be small and bounded.
3. **Level C - drift-adjusted inference:** a probability distribution produced by an ensemble transport model between the observation and SAR times.
4. **Level D - contextual candidate:** a front, roughness anomaly or broad proxy indicating where debris may occur, but insufficient for use as a supervised positive label.

Each label should retain its source, source and SAR timestamps, time difference, geometry, confidence level, environmental forcing, drift-model configuration and uncertainty estimate. Level D candidates remain valuable for data discovery, hard-negative review or self-supervised sampling, but treating them as confirmed positives would collapse the distinction between a physical accumulation process and plastic itself.

The hierarchy also clarifies how samples can be used. Level A labels are the preferred basis for independent evaluation because material and timing are most defensible. Level B labels can support training where the displacement tolerance is documented. Level C labels are suitable for confidence-weighted or soft-label experiments but should not dominate the test set. Level D candidates are not positive ground truth. They can guide scene selection, retrieval or manual inspection and may supply difficult negatives if independent evidence rejects plastic.

Negative labels require their own evidential standard. The absence of a visible target in one optical image does not prove that no plastic was present in the corresponding SAR pixel, especially under cloud, glint or sub-pixel conditions. High-confidence negatives should be areas where the relevant sensor could have observed an accumulation of the target scale and none was identified, or areas containing a confirmed confuser such as a vessel, natural windrow or infrastructure. Uncertain unlabelled water can be retained as an ignore class rather than forced into the negative class.

Annotation procedures should be repeatable. MARIDA's use of three experts, confidence levels and an inter-annotator agreement protocol illustrates how uncertainty can be retained rather than erased (Kikaki et al., 2022). A smaller SAR project may not support three independent annotators for every scene, but a second review of all positives and a stratified subset of negatives would still reveal systematic differences in boundary placement and class interpretation. The annotation guide should define the minimum target scale, treatment of mixed pixels, distinction between plastic and generic floating matter and circumstances that trigger an ignore label.

Label validation should compare multiple lines of evidence. A drift-adjusted target can be checked for consistency with the SAR anomaly, optical observations before and after the acquisition, independent current or front indicators and known lookalikes. Manual SAR interpretation is not independent confirmation of plastic, because it examines the same signal used by the model, but it can identify gross misregistration or an obvious ship, land edge or noise feature. The benefit of drift adjustment should be tested by comparing labels and model outcomes with and without the correction. Centroid displacement and polygon overlap can quantify transport alignment where a later independent observation exists. Maximum time difference should be varied in a sensitivity analysis rather than selected solely for the number of labels it produces.

Provenance must remain machine-readable throughout this process. At minimum, each label record should identify the original evidence item, acquisition identifiers, coordinate reference system, registration procedure, observation times, time offset, drift direction, forcing products, ensemble parameters, annotation version, reviewer and confidence level. Derived patches should retain links to this parent record. This makes it possible to exclude one event cleanly, regenerate a label after improved forcing becomes available and audit whether a reported test sample is genuinely independent.

This approach makes uncertainty part of the dataset rather than an unreported source of noise. It also provides the bridge between the observational literature and the related machine-learning studies: the model can only be evaluated meaningfully if the origin and confidence of each target are known.

## 2.8 Related Work

Research on remote detection of marine plastic has developed along several partly separate paths. Optical studies have produced the most mature labelled datasets and deep-learning workflows. Radar studies have explored direct scattering from controlled targets, large accumulations in constrained environments and indirect roughness proxies. A smaller body of work combines detection with physical transport modelling. Positioning the present study requires drawing these strands together without treating their outputs as interchangeable.

### 2.8.1 Optical Detection and Data-Centric Segmentation

In the optical domain, the Plastic Litter Projects established controlled targets for relating known plastic materials to airborne and Sentinel-2 observations (Topouzelis et al., 2020). Mifdal et al. (2021) subsequently demonstrated that CNN and U-Net models could learn spatial patterns in multi-regional Sentinel-2 floating-object data, while also exposing the limitations of coarse labels and geographically correlated patches. MARIDA expanded the task to multiclass segmentation and documented the confidence and provenance of annotations across diverse coastal scenes (Kikaki et al., 2022). Its explicit confuser classes show that a debris detector must operate among natural organic material, ships, wakes, foam, turbid water and cloud effects rather than against an ideal open-water background.

More recent optical work has shifted attention from architecture alone to data quality and operational context. Rußwurm et al. (2023) combined FloatingObjects, MARIDA and ship examples, refined labels and deliberately sampled difficult negatives. Their results indicate that harmonising heterogeneous labels and improving negative coverage can be more important than changing the segmentation architecture. Booth et al. (2023) used a U-Net-based workflow to map macroplastic density from high-resolution imagery, demonstrating the value of preserving spatial predictions rather than reducing a scene to a presence or absence label. These studies provide important design principles, but their learned spectral relationships cannot simply be transferred to radar.

Three transferable lessons emerge from this optical work. First, spatial context is valuable because debris often appears as elongated accumulations, fragments or windrows rather than isolated spectrally pure pixels. Second, explicit confusers and difficult negative examples are necessary for a detector intended to operate beyond curated positive scenes. Third, dataset semantics determine the meaning of the output: a model trained on generic floating-object lines does not become a material-specific plastic detector by changing the architecture. These lessons inform the SAR dataset and validation design even though the underlying sensor response is different.

The optical studies also reveal a scale problem. Controlled targets or high-resolution images can support comparatively precise object geometry, whereas Sentinel-2 debris is frequently sub-pixel or mixed with water. A network may learn the spatial and spectral expression of an accumulation without identifying each item within it. The corresponding SAR task is similarly an accumulation-detection problem. It should not be described as counting individual plastic objects or estimating concentration unless an independent calibration relationship is established.

### 2.8.2 Radar Detection: Proxy, Direct Response and Controlled Evidence

The SAR literature remains smaller and more heterogeneous. Davaasuren et al. (2018) and Evans and Ruf (2022) infer potential microplastic accumulation through large-scale reductions in radar-observed surface roughness. These methods may support regional screening, but they detect a physical proxy associated with films and surfactants rather than plastic material directly. Savastano et al. (2021) moved closer to direct coastal classification by transferring optical evidence to Sentinel-1 and applying shallow machine-learning models. The work is an important feasibility study, although its small dataset and temporal alignment problem limit conclusions about deep, scene-level segmentation.

Simpson et al. (2022) provided direct spaceborne evidence that large plastic islands in river environments can alter Sentinel-1 backscatter, and showed that cross-polarised and coherent SLC-derived information can improve discrimination. Qi et al. (2022) expanded the interpretation problem by showing that marine debris may appear bright or dark, whereas dense macroalgae more consistently produces strong backscatter. Controlled studies by de Fockert et al. (2024) and Nunziata et al. (2026) then isolated how concentration, target type, wave state and natural material affect detectability. Collectively, this work rejects a universal radar threshold and supports a multi-feature model trained with environmental and target-level diversity.

The radar studies differ along three critical dimensions. The first is the **inference target**: broad microplastic-associated roughness anomaly, persistent macroplastic accumulation or known experimental target. The second is the **measurement configuration**: bistatic roughness proxy, Sentinel-1 C-band VV/VH, or very high-resolution X- and Ku-band observations. The third is the **background environment**: open ocean, river barrier, hydrodynamic tank or calm lake. Results can be combined at the level of physical interpretation, but performance figures and thresholds cannot be pooled as though the studies measured the same phenomenon.

This distinction resolves an apparent contradiction in the literature. Dark anomalies are plausible where surfactants damp short waves over a broad area, while bright anomalies are plausible where macroplastic or associated debris increases roughness or adds scattering geometry. A coastal Sentinel-1 model may encounter both mechanisms as well as natural versions of both. Training it to detect "bright plastic" or "dark plastic" alone would encode a subset of the literature as a universal rule. The more defensible aim is to test whether a combination of backscatter, polarisation, texture, spatial form and context can distinguish labelled candidate accumulations from representative alternatives.

The most recent controlled radar evidence also sharpens the false-positive problem. Nunziata et al. (2026) found strong target-water separation under calm X-band conditions, but intensity-based dual-polarisation features did not straightforwardly separate plastic from natural foliage. This result is especially relevant because it shows that adding a second polarisation does not automatically create material specificity. The project must include natural floating matter as a difficult negative and report residual confusion rather than interpret every detected accumulation as confirmed plastic.

### 2.8.3 Self-Supervised SAR Representation Learning

Self-supervised SAR research provides a methodological bridge between abundant Sentinel-1 acquisitions and scarce plastic labels. Pei et al. (2023) trained a contrastive encoder on unlabelled SAR targets, mapped images into a discriminative embedding space and transferred the encoder to downstream classification with fewer labelled samples. Glaser et al. (2024) trained WV-Net on almost ten million Sentinel-1 Wave Mode images and showed that its domain-specific embeddings transferred better than a comparable ImageNet-pretrained model to several ocean and geophysical tasks.

These studies support the use of unlabelled SAR data, but they do not validate the full proposed pipeline. Pei et al. addressed target classification, and WV-Net used Wave Mode open-ocean imagettes rather than coastal Interferometric Wide Swath SLC feature stacks. The spatial scale, acquisition mode, channel structure and downstream objective differ from marine-plastic segmentation. Local pretraining is therefore justified as an experiment in representation transfer, with random initialisation and conventional pretraining retained as baselines. Its scientific contribution lies in testing whether local unlabelled coastal variation improves held-out segmentation, not in assuming that any contrastive embedding is inherently useful.

Embeddings may provide secondary value beyond weight initialisation. They can support retrieval of SAR patches similar to a confirmed event, identify clusters dominated by vessels, slicks or fronts and reveal whether labelled positives occupy a distinct or overlapping region of feature space. Such analyses are exploratory and cannot confirm unlabelled plastic, but they can improve dataset audit and hard-negative discovery. They may also expose a failure mode in which embeddings cluster mainly by incidence angle, location or acquisition date, indicating that the representation has learned nuisance variation instead of transferable ocean structure.

### 2.8.4 Detection Combined with Transport Modelling

Parallel research on transport modelling addresses the temporal gap between observations. Reviews of marine-plastic transport describe the combined influence of currents, winds, waves, convergence and object properties (van Sebille et al., 2020). OpenDrift-based studies demonstrate how these processes can be represented as particle trajectories with stochastic uncertainty (Tran et al., 2025). DEEP-PLAST combines optical segmentation and drift prediction in an integrated monitoring concept (Cernian and Iliuta, 2025), but its validation remains constrained by limited ground truth. These studies support drift-aware label transfer while also showing why a projected location should be represented probabilistically.

Transport and detection can be linked in two different directions. In an operational forecast, a detected target becomes the release location for future trajectories. In label construction, an earlier or later confirming observation is transported to the SAR acquisition time. The latter is more sensitive to uncertainty because the trajectory determines which pixels are presented to the model as truth. DEEP-PLAST demonstrates the attraction of coupling segmentation and Lagrangian prediction, but its drift validation remains primarily qualitative (Cernian and Iliuta, 2025). The appropriate contribution for the present study is therefore not a claim that drift produces exact ground truth. It is a comparison of unadjusted and ensemble-adjusted labels, with performance reported across confidence levels.

The transport literature also explains why South African locations should not be pooled without analysis. Currents, winds, waves, river discharge and coastal geometry create seasonally and regionally different pathways. Tran et al. (2025) show in another regional setting that seasonal forcing changes whether particles remain nearshore or disperse offshore. While those Vietnam-specific results cannot be transferred to South Africa, the general lesson is directly relevant: a model or label strategy evaluated in one circulation regime may not generalise to another.

### 2.8.5 Synthesis and Reproducibility

The consulted literature contains the components needed for a SAR-based investigation, but not their integration into an established benchmark. Optical studies contribute annotation and learning practices; controlled radar work contributes physical mechanisms; Sentinel-1 pilots demonstrate feasibility; self-supervised SAR studies contribute a response to scarce labels; and transport models contribute spatiotemporal alignment. None of these components compensates for weakness in another. A physically plausible feature cannot repair an incorrect label, a sophisticated model cannot create material specificity, and a drift simulation cannot confirm that the source anomaly was plastic.

The present project is positioned at the intersection of these strands. Its distinguishing combination is local Sentinel-1 SLC-derived feature stacks, an explicit hierarchy of confirming evidence, ensemble treatment of temporal displacement, self-supervised local pretraining, dense segmentation and geographic evaluation across three South African coastal settings. The claim should remain qualified: the consulted library contains very few direct Sentinel-1 marine-plastic studies and no established open deep-segmentation benchmark matching this combination. This statement is narrower and more defensible than claiming that SAR or deep learning has never been used for marine litter.

Reproducibility is part of the research gap. Reported performance depends on product type, preprocessing, target definition, patch sampling, negative selection and split unit. A reusable benchmark should therefore version source acquisitions and labels, preserve parent-event identifiers, publish or document split lists, record preprocessing and provide evaluation code. These records allow later work to distinguish a genuine model improvement from a change in data leakage, target semantics or operating threshold. They also make negative or null findings useful, particularly if candidate plastic cannot be separated reliably from natural floating matter.

The related work therefore supports a specific next step rather than a broad promise of automated ocean cleanup: construct and evaluate a physically informed, uncertainty-aware SAR dataset in which every claimed improvement can be traced to an input feature, training strategy or labelling decision. The unresolved elements of that step define the research gaps below.

## 2.9 Research Gaps and Position of the Present Study

Six connected gaps emerge from the literature. They are methodological as well as observational, and each requires a testable project response.

First, the radar response of marine plastic is physically variable. Bright direct returns from dense floating material and dark surfactant-related proxies have both been reported, while wind, waves, target geometry and concentration change the observed contrast. A single backscatter threshold is therefore unlikely to generalise across coastal scenes.

The project response is to represent the radar observation through complementary feature families rather than one intensity value. VV and VH describe co- and cross-polarised backscatter; their ratio or difference captures relative channel behaviour; local texture describes spatial organisation; and partial dual-polarisation descriptors summarise covariance structure. This response is testable through feature-group ablation. If the additional descriptors do not improve independent scenes beyond calibrated VV/VH, they should not be retained merely because they are physically sophisticated.

Second, the same SAR features are produced by common lookalikes. Ships, wakes, macroalgae, natural debris, fronts, oil or biogenic films, low-wind areas, breaking waves and coastal infrastructure can all resemble some aspect of a candidate plastic signature. Existing plastic-focused radar studies do not yet provide a large multiclass benchmark containing this range of confusers.

The project response is to construct background data deliberately rather than sample generic water at random. Hard negatives should represent both target-level confusers and acquisition artefacts. Confusion should be reported by negative category and scene, allowing a result such as "floating accumulation detected but material unresolved" to remain visible. Environmental variables can explain expected roughness and transport context, but they cannot convert a radar anomaly into material confirmation.

Third, labelled data are scarce and uneven. Optical datasets are considerably more developed than SAR datasets, while direct radar evidence is concentrated in small pilots, controlled experiments or river and lake environments. The consulted literature does not provide an established multi-location Sentinel-1 SLC segmentation dataset for floating marine plastic in South African coastal waters.

The project response is a provenance-rich local dataset centred on Durban, Gqeberha and Cape Town. These locations provide contrasting coastal, urban and oceanographic settings rather than interchangeable samples from one distribution. The contribution includes not only positive masks but source acquisitions, event identifiers, evidence class, hard negatives and split definitions. A dataset of fewer but well-audited independent events is scientifically preferable to a larger patch count created by repeatedly cropping the same scenes.

Fourth, temporal mismatch between a confirming observation and the SAR acquisition is often treated as an alignment inconvenience rather than a source of label uncertainty. Debris transport can displace a target substantially, and a deterministic trajectory does not capture uncertainty in environmental forcing or object behaviour. Labels need provenance, confidence and probabilistic drift envelopes.

The project response is to retain direct, near-coincident, drift-adjusted and contextual evidence as separate levels. Ensemble trajectories can propagate uncertainty in release position, windage and forcing to the SAR time. Their endpoint density produces a conditional location surface, not exact ground truth. The scientific test is whether drift adjustment improves consistency with independent observations and held-out model performance relative to unadjusted transfer. If it only spreads labels until they overlap more predictions, the apparent improvement should not be interpreted as better alignment.

Fifth, limited labels make supervised training from random initialisation vulnerable to overfitting and geographic bias. Self-supervised SAR representation learning is well developed in adjacent remote-sensing applications, but has not been fully connected to uncertainty-aware Sentinel-1 segmentation of marine plastic.

The project response is to pretrain an encoder on unlabelled local Sentinel-1 data and then fine-tune it within a segmentation network. The embedding is expected to capture common coastal SAR structure before rare target labels are introduced. This remains a hypothesis. Random initialisation, generic pretraining where technically compatible and local self-supervision should be compared on identical downstream splits. Embedding retrieval or clustering can provide diagnostic evidence, but only held-out segmentation can establish utility for the target task.

Sixth, reported model performance can be inflated by patch overlap, background dominance and unrecognised geographic domain shift. A high pixel accuracy on randomly mixed patches does not show that the method transfers to a new acquisition or coastline. Existing optical studies already identify scene correlation and regional domain shift as major limitations (Mifdal et al., 2021; Kikaki et al., 2022; Rußwurm et al., 2023).

The project response is a hierarchy of scene-, event- and location-disjoint tests. Precision, recall, F1 score, intersection over union and precision-recall behaviour should be accompanied by false candidate regions per scene and performance by confidence and environmental stratum. Holding out each of Durban, Gqeberha and Cape Town in turn would test geographic transfer. Probability calibration and uncertainty-aware evaluation prevent a high-confidence output from being mistaken for equally high evidential certainty.

The present study is designed to examine these gaps through a reproducible SAR training dataset and machine-learning framework for the South African Exclusive Economic Zone, with focused evaluation around the three selected coastal regions. Sentinel-1 SLC observations form the principal sensor source. Backscatter, inter-polarisation, texture and dual-polarisation descriptors represent multiple aspects of the radar return, while wind, wave and current information provide physical context. Labels are assembled from direct and optical evidence with explicit provenance, and temporal offsets are handled through ensemble drift modelling and confidence-aware target surfaces.

An Attention U-Net is proposed for pixel-wise segmentation because it combines multi-scale spatial prediction with gates that may suppress irrelevant coastal background. Its encoder is first exposed to unlabelled local SAR imagery through self-supervised learning and is then fine-tuned using the smaller labelled collection. A conventional U-Net and simpler baselines are required to determine whether attention adds value. Evaluation is separated by scene, event and location and reports rare-class and scene-level performance, together with results across label-confidence and environmental strata.

The intended contribution is not an operational claim that SAR uniquely identifies plastic polymer. It does not estimate all plastic in the water column, count individual items below the effective resolution, derive mass concentration without calibration or assume that a non-detection means absence. It is a physically informed and reproducible investigation of whether candidate floating-plastic accumulations can be distinguished from realistic coastal backgrounds under known uncertainty.

A scientifically useful outcome does not require uniformly high accuracy. If performance is restricted to calm water, dense accumulations or one location, the study should report that operating envelope. If natural debris remains inseparable, the valid output may be a broader floating-accumulation candidate class requiring optical or field confirmation. If self-supervision, environmental fusion or SLC-derived features do not improve independent scenes, those negative results still constrain future system design. This framing aligns the capabilities of SAR, the limitations of the available evidence and the methodological contribution of the master's project.
